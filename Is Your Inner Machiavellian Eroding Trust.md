Is Your Inner Machiavellian Eroding Your Trust Quotient?
A practical framework for measuring strategic leadership, political behavior, and organizational trust.


One of the challenges with Machiavellian tendencies is that **people high in Machiavellianism often believe they are simply being "realistic" or "strategic."** As a result, self-assessment requires looking beyond your intentions and examining how others consistently experience working with you.

## The Three Dimensions to Measure

Think of Machiavellianism as three interacting dimensions:

| Dimension   | Healthy Strategic Leadership     | High Machiavellianism                                        |
| ----------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Intent**  | Advance the organization's goals | Preserve influence, status, or control                       |
| **Methods** | Transparent persuasion           | Manipulation, withholding information, political maneuvering |
| **Impact**  | Trust increases over time        | Trust declines despite good results                          |

If results improve but trust steadily declines, that's an important signal.

# Self-Assessment (Rate 1–5)

**1 = Never**
**5 = Almost Always**

### A. Power & Control

* I like knowing information others don't.
* I intentionally control who gets what information.
* I prefer decisions ultimately depend on me.
* I dislike becoming replaceable.
* I cultivate relationships partly because they may be useful later.

Subtotal: **25**

---

### B. Influence & Persuasion

* I tell different stakeholders slightly different versions of the same story.
* I sometimes omit information to obtain a better outcome.
* I adapt my personality significantly depending on whom I'm speaking with.
* I think politics are necessary to accomplish important goals.
* I believe the end often justifies the means.

Subtotal: **25**

---

### C. Empathy

* I understand people's emotions well.
* I sometimes use that understanding to influence them.
* I rarely feel guilty for making difficult political decisions.
* I can separate personal feelings from organizational objectives.
* I find emotional attachment can interfere with leadership.

Subtotal: **25**

---

### D. Transparency

* I openly explain why decisions are made.
* I admit mistakes quickly.
* People usually know where they stand with me.
* I share bad news promptly.
* I encourage disagreement.

Reverse score these.

Subtotal: **25**

# Trust Impact Assessment

Ask yourself:

### Psychological Safety

Would people tell me...

* I made a mistake?
* They disagree?
* They need help?
* Bad news?

If "No," trust is already declining.

---

### Transparency

Do people know:

* Why decisions are made?
* What success looks like?
* Why priorities change?

If not, uncertainty often grows.

---

### Predictability

Can employees predict:

* How I'll react?
* What values guide my decisions?
* Whether I'll support them?

High predictability generally strengthens trust.

---

### Fairness

Would different employees say:

> "Everyone plays by the same rules."

If not, perceptions of favoritism emerge.

---

### Psychological Contract

Ask yourself:

Have I ever...

* promised autonomy but micromanaged?
* encouraged debate then punished disagreement?
* praised transparency but rewarded politics?
* claimed "people first" while consistently putting deadlines first?

Each inconsistency weakens trust.

# The Trust Equation

A useful framework is:

**Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) ÷ Self-Orientation**

Where:

* **Credibility** = Competence
* **Reliability** = Consistency
* **Intimacy** = Psychological safety
* **Self-Orientation** = Perceived focus on yourself rather than the team

Leaders with stronger Machiavellian tendencies often score well on credibility but may see trust reduced if others perceive high self-orientation.

# Observable Warning Signs

Instead of focusing only on your intentions, watch for outcomes like:

| Observation                                | Possible Interpretation             |
| ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------- |
| Meetings become unusually quiet            | People don't feel safe disagreeing. |
| Bad news arrives late                      | Fear outweighs openness.            |
| High performers leave despite good pay     | Trust or autonomy may be lacking.   |
| Decisions are frequently second-guessed    | Rationale isn't understood.         |
| Employees seek approval for small issues   | They feel they lack real authority. |
| People agree publicly but resist privately | Compliance has replaced commitment. |

# A 360° Feedback Exercise

Invite anonymous ratings (1–10) from your manager, peers, and direct reports on questions such as:

* I trust this leader to act consistently.
* This leader explains important decisions.
* I feel safe disagreeing with this leader.
* This leader shares credit fairly.
* This leader treats people consistently.
* This leader uses influence ethically.
* This leader puts the organization's interests ahead of personal interests.
* This leader follows the same rules expected of others.

Compare how you rate yourself with how others rate you. Large gaps often reveal blind spots.

---

# The Goal: Strategic Without Becoming Manipulative

Being politically aware or adaptable is not inherently negative. Many effective leaders switch between directive, participative, coaching, and delegating styles as situations demand. The distinction lies in whether your influence is perceived as **transparent, fair, and aligned with the organization's interests**, or whether people increasingly believe it serves your own influence first.

A good sign you're balancing strategy with trust is when people continue to bring you bad news early, challenge your ideas respectfully, and support decisions even when they disagree—because they believe your process is fair and your motives are aligned with the team's success.
